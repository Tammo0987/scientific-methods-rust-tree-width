\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Control-flow graphs (CFGs) are a fundamental abstraction used by compilers to represent the execution structure of programs, and many optimisation and analysis tasks operate directly on them~\cite{allen_1970}.
A key structural property of such graphs is \emph{treewidth}, a parameter that measures how tree-like a graph is~\cite{bodlaender_1993}.
Graphs with small treewidth admit linear-time solutions to many otherwise NP-hard problems, making the treewidth of CFGs a meaningful question for compiler design (see Section~\ref{sec:background})~\cite{thorup_1998}.

Register allocation is one such optimisation problem: if the underlying CFG has bounded treewidth, dynamic programming can be used to compute optimal allocations efficiently~\cite{thorup_1998}.
Understanding the structural complexity of real-world CFGs therefore has direct implications for the feasibility of exact optimisation techniques in compilers~\cite{bodlaender_1988}.

Thorup established that CFGs of goto-free C programs have a treewidth of at most six, connecting structured programming directly to bounded treewidth~\cite{thorup_1998}.
Building on this, Gustedt et al. investigated whether the same holds in practice for Java, finding a theoretical bound of at most six for label-free Java and confirming empirically that real Java methods exhibit small treewidth values, with an average of 2.7 and a maximum of five in the Java standard library~\cite{gustedt_2002}.

Based on these results, we raise a similar question for modern programming languages: \textit{what is the treewidth of Rust programs?}
Rather than analysing source-level control flow, we operate on the Mid-level Intermediate Representation (MIR), the compiler's internal CFG representation on which our measurements are based.

By intercepting the Rust compiler pipeline after MIR optimisation, we extract the CFG of each function and compute its treewidth using FlowCutter.
This measures structural complexity at the same level at which the compiler performs its analyses.

Our contributions are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Tool:} We implement \texttt{mir-extractor}, a custom
	\texttt{rustc} compiler driver that extracts MIR CFGs as undirected graphs
	and computes their treewidth via \texttt{FlowCutter}, providing a reusable
	pipeline for MIR-level structural analysis of Rust programs.
	\item \textbf{Empirical baseline:} We measure the treewidth of all
	functions in the Rust standard library, establishing that MIR CFGs have
	very small treewidth in practice.
	\item \textbf{Safe/unsafe comparison:} We show that unsafe Rust functions
	do not exhibit higher structural complexity than safe functions.
	\item \textbf{Contextualisation:} We situate our results within Thorup's
	framework and Gustedt et al.'s Java study, and explain why a formal bound
	analogous to Thorup's cannot be derived at the MIR level.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows.
We begin by introducing the necessary background on CFGs and treewidth (Section~\ref{sec:background}) and reviewing prior work on the treewidth of structured programs (Section~\ref{sec:related-work}).
We then provide background on Rust and MIR (Section~\ref{sec:rust-and-mir}) before describing the methodology for extracting CFGs and computing their treewidth (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}).
Finally, we present and discuss the results (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}) and summarise the findings with directions for future work (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}).
