\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\subsection{Treewidth of Structured Programs}
\label{subsec:rw-treewidth-structured}
In earlier work, Thorup~\cite{thorup_1998} analyses the treewidth of CFGs from different structured programs and shows that those graphs have bounded treewidths. The main idea of his research is that structured programs have no \texttt{goto} jumps, which restricts the control flow and therefore limits the complexity of their CFGs. In particular, Theorem 1 shows that goto-free Algol and Pascal programs have a treewidth of $\leq3$, all Modula-2 programs have a treewidth of $\leq5$ and goto-free C programs have a treewidth of $\leq6$, assuming all languages have \texttt{short-circuit evaluation}.

Thorup’s approach to calculating these treewidths is based on k-complex listings of program statements and a corresponding ordering of CFG nodes. This ensures that each node has a listing of separators of size at most $k$ consisting of the node's neighbors. Then, by visiting the statements in the structured programs, and computing their separators, the resulting CFG can be shown to have bounded treewidth.

In the case of goto-free C programs, the higher bound of 6 arises from the presence of \texttt{continue} statements in loops, which expand the separator listings of neighbors. Whereas, Modula-2 programs do not have \texttt{continue} statements, and therefore have a treewidth of $\leq5$.

Thorup's results provide a theoretical basis on analysing structured programs, showing they have bounded treewidths. This is relevant for our work, as it establishes how language specific control-flow constructs influence CFG complexity, motivating the study of bounded treewidth in modern programming languages, such as Rust.

\subsection{Treewidth of Java Programs}
\label{subsec:rw-java}

Building on Thorup's theoretical results for structured programs~\cite{thorup_1998}, Gustedt, M{\ae}hle, and Telle~\cite{gustedt_2002} investigate whether the same treewidth bounds apply to Java.
Thorup showed that \texttt{goto}-free C programs have CFG treewidth at most~6, using the notion of \emph{flow-affecting constructs} (FACs): language constructs that add non-local control-flow edges and increase treewidth by at most~1 each.

Although Java has no \texttt{goto} statement, its labeled \texttt{break} and \texttt{continue} statements are expressively equivalent to \texttt{goto}.
Consequently, unrestricted Java programs can have arbitrarily high treewidth.
Restricting to a label-free subset eliminates this issue: label-free Java has exactly four FACs (\texttt{break}, \texttt{continue}, \texttt{return}, and \texttt{short-circuit evaluation}), yielding a theoretical treewidth bound of at most~$2 + 4 = 6$.

To complement this theoretical bound, the authors implement a tool to compute tree decompositions of Java CFGs and apply it to 9387 methods from the Java standard library.
The average treewidth is~2.7 and the maximum observed is~5, with over 95\% of methods having treewidth at most~3, well below the theoretical bound.

This paper is directly relevant to our work: it establishes both a theoretical treewidth bound and empirical baselines for a real-world language, serving as the primary comparison point for our analogous analysis of Rust programs via MIR.
