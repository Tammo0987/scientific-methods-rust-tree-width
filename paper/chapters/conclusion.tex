\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\subsection{Summary}
\label{subsec:conclusion-summary}

To evaluate the structural complexity of Rust programs, we have measured the treewidth of 27{,}997 monomorphised MIR CFGs from the Rust standard library (\texttt{core}, \texttt{alloc}, \texttt{std}).
The mean treewidth is 1.04, the median is 1, and the maximum observed value is 6.
Moreover, 99.65\% of all functions have treewidth at most~3, and all functions have treewidth at most~6.
Unsafe functions do not exhibit larger treewidth than safe functions.

These findings provide strong empirical evidence that practical Rust MIR CFGs are usually structurally simple and are, in almost all cases, close to trees.
The consistently small treewidth values suggest that treewidth-based compiler optimisations are practically applicable to real Rust code.
At the same time, the study is limited to the Rust standard library, a single compiler configuration, and MIR-level CFGs, so the results should be interpreted within that scope.

\subsection{Future Work}
\label{subsec:future-work}

These limitations noted in \ref{subsec:discussion} point to several directions for future work.
First, it would be valuable to extend the analysis beyond the Rust standard library to third-party crates and application code, in order to assess how broadly the observed low treewidth generalises.

Second, working at a higher level of abstraction, in particular Rust's HIR which remains closer to source structure, would enable a more direct comparison with the Java results of Gustedt et al.~\cite{gustedt_2002}.
It may also open the possibility of a structural decomposition in the spirit of Thorup's construction, which would establish nontrivial treewidth bounds and explain from first principles why treewidth remains small in practice~\cite{thorup_1998}.

Finally, a related direction is to study treewidth for restricted Rust fragments, such as programs without labeled breaks or async functions, where a Thorup-style bound may be provable by construction.
